One of us is a stay-at-home partner. How do we figure out their ‘fair share’ of household costs when they have no income?

Recognizing non-financial contributions and splitting expenses fairly

📅 Published: August 1, 2026 ⏱️ 8 min read

One partner works. The other stays home with the kids. Someone needs to pay the bills. But how do you split them fairly when only one person brings in a paycheck?

This is a common situation. According to research from Royal London Ireland, the estimated annual value of a stay-at-home parent's work is now over €60,000 [1]. Yet more than eight in ten people still underestimate this value [1].

The short answer? You don't split bills 50/50 when one partner stays home. That approach ignores the enormous value of the unpaid work being done. Instead, you need a system that recognizes both financial and non-financial contributions.

In this guide, we'll show you how to value a stay-at-home partner's contribution, practical ways to structure your finances, and how to have the conversation without awkwardness.

What Is a Stay-at-Home Parent Really Worth?

Let's start with some numbers. Research from Royal London Ireland calculated the estimated annual salary of a stay-at-home parent at €60,112 per year in 2026 [1]. That's up from €57,140 in 2024 and €40,560 in 2015, when the study first began [1][2].

This figure is based on the cost of hiring someone to perform all the tasks a stay-at-home parent does daily: childcare, cooking, cleaning, household management, transportation, and more [2][3].

Yet public perception lags far behind. More than 8 in 10 people underestimate the financial value of stay-at-home parents [1]. One in four adults believe it would cost just €20,000 to €30,000 to replace their work [1]. And 6% believe the cost would be under €10,000 [4].

The point isn't that you should literally pay your partner €60,000 a year. The point is that their work has real, significant value. Recognizing this is the first step to fair expense splitting.

The Non-Financial Contribution: What It Includes

When one partner stays home, they're making a non-financial contribution to the household. These are tasks and efforts that don't involve earning money but still add enormous value.

Common non-financial contributions include:

  • Childcare: Feeding, bathing, dressing, educating, and caring for children throughout the day.
  • Household management: Cleaning, cooking, laundry, grocery shopping, and home maintenance.
  • Transportation: Driving children to school, activities, appointments, and social events.
  • Emotional support: Providing stability, nurturing, and guidance for the whole family.
  • Career support: Enabling the working partner to focus on their career without worrying about home duties.

As Karen O'Flaherty, Senior Propositions Executive of Royal London Ireland, put it: "Stay-at-home parents have a diverse range of responsibilities – including childcare, household management, budgeting, emotional support, and handling children's transportation needs – yet the full value of their work often goes unrecognised" [2][5].

This recognition matters not just for legal purposes, but for how couples view each other's worth in daily life.

Why 50/50 Doesn't Work in a Single-Income Household

Splitting bills 50/50 assumes both partners have income to contribute. When one partner stays home, that's not the case.

Asking a stay-at-home parent to pay half the bills from no income is impossible. Even if they have savings, it's unsustainable and unfair.

Here's why the 50/50 approach fails in this scenario:

  • It ignores the value of unpaid work. The stay-at-home partner is contributing labor worth over €60,000 annually [1].
  • It creates a power imbalance. The partner with income controls the finances, while the stay-at-home partner becomes financially dependent.
  • It breeds resentment. The stay-at-home partner may feel undervalued and trapped.
  • It's unsustainable. Over time, the stay-at-home partner's savings deplete, leaving them with no financial independence.

How to Value the Contribution Fairly

So if 50/50 doesn't work, what does? Here are practical approaches to valuing a stay-at-home partner's contribution.

1. Treat Household Income as Shared

The simplest and most common approach is to treat all income as family income. Both partners have equal access to and decision-making power over the household finances.

Under this model, there's no "splitting" of bills. The household income pays for household expenses. Both partners are equally responsible for budgeting and financial decisions.

2. "Pay" the Stay-at-Home Partner

Some couples create a regular "paycheck" for the stay-at-home partner. This is a monthly transfer into an account in their name for personal spending and savings.

As one financial expert explains, creating financial independence for the non-earning spouse needs to be intentional. A simple way to reduce power imbalances is a deliberate monthly transfer into an account in the stay-at-home parent's name for personal spending, plus a long-term savings or investment account that builds assets over time.

Example

Monthly household income: $6,000

Household expenses: $4,000

Remaining: $2,000

Stay-at-home partner receives: $800/month for personal spending and savings

Working partner receives: $800/month for personal spending and savings

Remaining $400 goes to joint savings

3. Calculate the Market Value of Their Work

Another approach is to calculate what it would cost to hire someone to do the stay-at-home partner's tasks. Research from Royal London Ireland breaks this down by task [3]:

  • Childcare Provider: €14.89 per hour (30 hours/week) = €446.70 weekly
  • Cleaner: €12.93 per hour (8 hours/week) = €103.44 weekly
  • General Cook: €13.76 per hour (15 hours/week) = €206.40 weekly
  • Teaching Assistant: €20.10 per hour (5 hours/week) = €100.50 weekly
  • Handyman: €19.86 per hour (4 hours/week) = €79.44 weekly
  • Taxi Driver: €14.80 per trip (10 trips/week) = €148 weekly

The total weekly cost: €1,098.85. The total yearly cost: €57,140.20 [3]. In 2026, this increased to over €60,000 [1].

While you wouldn't literally pay this amount, it helps both partners understand the economic value of the unpaid work being done.

4. Build Assets in Both Names

A common gap is that long-term investments and savings sit in the working partner's name simply because that's where the income lands. To correct this, allocate part of monthly savings into investment accounts owned by the stay-at-home partner.

This builds financial independence and ensures both partners have identifiable assets.

Retirement and Long-Term Protection

One of the biggest risks for stay-at-home parents is the loss of retirement savings. When one partner isn't earning, they're also not contributing to a pension or Social Security.

Research from Harvard Kennedy School found that women who reduced work hours after childbirth saw a 35% dip in overall lifetime earnings and a 43% loss in retirement savings [6].

To protect the stay-at-home partner's long-term financial security:

  • Contribute to a spousal IRA or pension. Many countries allow contributions to a retirement account in the non-working partner's name.
  • Build a financial footprint. Ensure the stay-at-home partner has a bank account, credit history, and financial identity in their own name.
  • Get life and disability insurance. Both partners should be insured, including coverage that accounts for the value of the stay-at-home parent's unpaid work.

As Karen O'Flaherty noted, the role of a stay-at-home parent should be recognized not as an invisible contribution, but as an essential job that deserves both support and consideration in financial planning [7].

How to Have the Conversation

Talking about money when one partner stays home can feel sensitive. Here's how to approach it the best way.

1. Start with appreciation

Begin by acknowledging the value of your partner's work. A sincere "I appreciate everything you do for our family" sets a positive tone.

2. Frame it as a team conversation

Use "we" language. "We need to figure out a system that works for both of us" is better than "I need you to spend less."

3. Look at the numbers together

Use a calculator to see how different approaches would work. Our Couples Expense Fair Split Calculator can show you side-by-side comparisons.

4. Be open to adjusting

You might not get it right on the first try. Check in regularly and adjust as needed.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do you split bills when one partner stays home with kids?

You don't split 50/50. Instead, treat household income as shared, or create a system where the stay-at-home partner receives a regular "paycheck" for their contributions. The key is recognizing the value of their unpaid work.

What is a stay-at-home parent's work worth financially?

Research from Royal London Ireland calculated the annual value at over €60,000 in 2026, based on the cost of hiring someone to do all the tasks a stay-at-home parent performs daily [1].

Should a stay-at-home parent get a salary from their partner?

Some couples choose to "pay" the stay-at-home partner a monthly amount for personal spending and savings. This helps maintain financial independence and reduces power imbalances. The amount should be agreed upon by both partners.

How do stay-at-home parents save for retirement?

Working partners can contribute to a spousal IRA or pension account in the stay-at-home partner's name. Many countries allow this even when one partner has no earned income. Research shows women who reduce work hours after childbirth can lose up to 43% in retirement savings [6].

What if the stay-at-home partner feels financially trapped?

This is a common concern. To address it, ensure the stay-at-home partner has their own bank account, credit history, and access to household funds. Regular check-ins and open communication about finances can also help.

How do you split bills if one partner stays home but has savings?

Savings shouldn't be treated as income for bill splitting. The stay-at-home partner's savings are their personal assets. Household bills should be paid from household income or from a system that values their non-financial contributions.

Is it fair for the working partner to control all the money?

No. Financial control should be shared. The stay-at-home partner should have equal access to and decision-making power over household finances. This is about respect and partnership, not just money.
